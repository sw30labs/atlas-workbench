# Provenance notes for two additions (2026-08-06)

## 1. WWII aircraft / missing bullet holes (survivorship bias)

**Status: include — provenance solid; popular retelling slightly simplified.**

- **Who:** Abraham Wald (1902–1950), mathematician; work done with the US **Statistical Research Group (SRG)** during WWII (Columbia-linked wartime statistical unit).
- **Claim used in article:** Returning aircraft showed denser damage on some surfaces (e.g. fuselage) than others (e.g. engines); armoring only where holes are densest confuses "damage that was survivable enough to return" with "where hits are most dangerous." The missing planes carry the missing holes.
- **Sources (secondary, well-established):** Jordan Ellenberg, *How Not to Be Wrong* (widely cited popular account); AMS Feature Column on Wald; multiple statistical pedagogy treatments of survivorship bias.
- **Caveats applied in prose:**
  - No invented military dialogue or "they only wanted to armor the fuselage" cartoon.
  - Framed as insight *associated with* Wald / SRG, not as a courtroom transcript.
  - Analogy to operational-risk checklists is interpretive (author's), not a historical claim that Wald worked on banks.

## 2. Pre-mortem

**Status: include — provenance solid.**

- **Who:** Gary Klein (cognitive psychologist / decision research).
- **Canonical popular source:** Gary Klein, "Performing a Project Premortem," *Harvard Business Review*, September 2007.
- **Intellectual root:** "Prospective hindsight" (Mitchell, Russo & Pennington, 1989, "Back to the Future…") — imagining a future outcome as already known improves generation of explanatory paths.
- **Claim used in article:** Opposite of post-mortem: assume the project/initiative has already failed; generate causes in past tense; remediate before launch; re-run when surface changes.
- **Caveats applied in prose:**
  - No claim that pre-mortem replaces enumeration engines or produces calibrated probabilities.
  - Did **not** assert the often-repeated "30% more risks identified" figure as hard fact in the essay (that figure is a popular gloss on the 1989 prospective-hindsight literature; useful in footnotes, easy to overclaim in body copy).
  - Five-step practice list is a faithful operationalization of Klein-style premortem for risk readers, not a verbatim HBR reprint.
